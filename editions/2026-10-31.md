## Egypt News

**Crude imports on supplier credit:** The Oil Ministry plans to import about 15 million barrels of crude in the fourth quarter, 8% more than a year earlier, and pay through supplier credit, according to EnterpriseAM citing Al Arabiya Business. The cargoes, from Saudi Arabia, Libya, Greece and other sources, are expected to cost $1.4 billion to $1.5 billion, with deliveries of 6 million barrels in October and 4.5 million in each of November and December. Egyptian refineries cover 70% to 80% of fuel demand, and the ministry wants to cut imports of refined products to $3.6 billion this year from $6.2 billion in the 2025/26 fiscal year. With Brent above $113, the bill is a direct pressure on the pound.

**Sisi and Putin in Moscow:** President Abdel Fattah El-Sisi met Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on Wednesday on the sidelines of the third Russia-Africa Summit, according to Daily News Egypt. Sisi reaffirmed Egypt's commitment to the El-Dabaa nuclear plant's timeline and payments, and raised the prospect of new reactors after 2030, plus a research reactor and small floating units. Putin said bilateral trade passed $10 billion in 2025 and rose 14% in the first eight months of this year, while more than 1.2 million Russian tourists visited Egypt in the first half. Rosatom separately said the "sabotage" its chief described meant misinformation campaigns against the project, and that construction at El-Dabaa remains on schedule.

**I-Score rule for consumer finance:** From April 2027, consumer finance companies must check an I-Score credit score before approving financing, under a new rule from the Financial Regulatory Authority, according to EnterpriseAM. The score draws on alternative data of the kind used by AI lending models. Fintechs are letting software make more of the decisions, while banks move more slowly, partly because central bank rules keep customer data off cloud AI services, two bank chief executives said. For borrowers, the rule should make it harder to take on credit from several lenders at once.

**Alexandria coastal protection:** Sixteen coastal protection projects have been carried out in Alexandria at a total cost of nearly EGP 5.6 billion, Irrigation Minister Hani Sewilam told a Cairo Water Week workshop. They include a EGP 1.1 billion submerged breakwater, work to restore about 8.5 kilometres of beaches between Abu Qir and the Eastern Harbour, and coastal risk maps extending to 2100. The city is among the Mediterranean coastal areas most exposed to rising seas and storm surge.

**Automated traffic enforcement:** Cairo is to get automated traffic enforcement "in the near future", traffic expert Ahmed Hisham said. The system, already in use in the New Administrative Capital and on roads towards New Alamein, detects speeding and violations that conventional radar and cameras can miss.

**EgyptAir to Birmingham:** EgyptAir has launched non-stop flights between Cairo and Birmingham four times a week, on Tuesdays, Thursdays, Fridays and Sundays, using Boeing 737 MAX 8 aircraft. It is the carrier's third UK destination after London Heathrow and Manchester.

## Worth Knowing

**Free cash flow and why heavy spending worries investors:** A company's free cash flow is the cash left after paying for the equipment and buildings it needs, known as capital expenditure. It is what remains for dividends, buybacks and debt repayment, and it is why Alphabet's fall this week mattered more than its profit.

Take an illustrative company that generates $100 of cash from operations and spends $110 on data centres. Its free cash flow is minus $10, so it must borrow or draw down savings to cover the gap. If the same company earns $100 and spends $60, free cash flow is $40, and it can pay shareholders and still invest. Profit can look healthy in both cases, because buildings and chips are written off over many years while the cash leaves at once.

Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion in the third quarter on capital spending of $51.4 billion, and expects up to $205 billion for the year. Investors accept heavy spending when it promises faster growth, and punish it when the old business slows, as search advertising growth did. Reading cash flow beside profit shows whether growth is being paid for from earnings or from borrowing.
