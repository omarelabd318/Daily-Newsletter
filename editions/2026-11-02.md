## Egypt News

**Domestic borrowing:** The government raised about EGP 4.3 trillion at home in the first four months of FY 2026/27, an official document shows: EGP 967 billion in July, 969 billion in August, 1.3 trillion in September and 1.08 trillion in October. Almost all of it refinances existing debt, because new issuance averages about a year in maturity and bills made up 85% of recent accepted issuance. A senior Finance Ministry official said local borrowing remains cheaper than the alternatives while international markets stay difficult, even after the 100-basis-point hike, which adds roughly EGP 137 billion a year on the EGP 13.68 trillion domestic stock by one estimate. Egypt also signed a $1 billion World Bank development policy financing agreement on Sunday, the second tranche of a three-part programme that pays out once reforms are legislated.

**Tax strategy:** Ramy Youssef, the deputy finance minister for tax policy, said that a flat levy of up to 4% on freezone goods sold into the local market will not be imposed before the IMF's eighth review closes, and remains under study. The new tax strategy goes out for public consultation before the end of the year. The VAT registration threshold stays at EGP 500,000, there is no plan for the OECD's 15% global minimum tax or a domestic carbon tax, and Egypt will not tax digital services unilaterally. A property tax mobile app is due this month.

**Priority land reservation:** Qualified Egyptian and foreign companies that commit to pay a plot's full price in dollars wired from abroad will skip price bidding and get priority on plots offered on instalments, under a mechanism the Housing Ministry announced for November's land offerings. Applicants register on the foreign-investment portal and wire at least $100,000 plus $5,500 by SWIFT; the plot is then booked provisionally, and failing to pay the balance within a month forfeits $100,000. Net foreign investment in Egyptian property reached $2.8 billion in FY 2025/26, up 47%.

**Technical schools:** President Abdel Fattah El Sisi reviewed plans for 100 Italian-backed technical schools, with the number to double within two years, and for Germany-supported centres of excellence at 50 more. Average student attendance reached 91.6% in the 2026/27 school year, and financial literacy and programming are being added to the curriculum. He also asked for more beds at university hospitals, including the 577-bed Galala Teaching Hospital, and for an international operator for the 340-bed cancer hospital planned in Sheikh Zayed.

**Solar for factories:** The government plans 1,000 megawatts of solar capacity to supply about 7,000 factories under the Shams El-Senaa initiative. The aim is lower electricity bills and less gas burned in industry, and Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly wants later phases to cover government buildings. Local manufacturing of solar components is part of the plan.

**Museum's first year:** The Grand Egyptian Museum drew roughly 5.2 to 5.3 million visitors in its first year, along with about 60 heads of state and rulers, its chief executive Ahmed Ghoneim said, with the final count due at the anniversary celebrations. Timed entry slots and crowd limits are in place, and a six-month immersive sound-and-light installation is running.

## Worth Knowing

**Rollover risk and average maturity:** A government that borrows for a short time must borrow again soon, and each new loan is priced at the rates of that day. Average maturity, the weighted length of its debts, measures how exposed it is.

Take an illustrative government with EGP 1,000 billion of debt. If it all falls due in three months at 20%, it must re-sell EGP 1,000 billion within a quarter, and if rates rise to 22% the extra annual cost is EGP 20 billion. If the same debt is spread over five years at a fixed 20%, only a fifth is re-sold each year, so a two-point rise adds about EGP 4 billion in the first year, building slowly as old debt matures.

This is why Egypt's Finance Ministry reports average maturity: new domestic issuance reached 1.1 years in April to June 2026, the highest in three years, according to its debt bulletin. Longer debt costs more per year in normal conditions but spreads the risk, and a rate hike hits the budget less quickly.
