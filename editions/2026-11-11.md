## Egypt News

**Troops in Saudi Arabia:** Egypt's foreign minister gave the first official confirmation that Egyptian forces are in Saudi Arabia. Badr Abdelatty told France 24 that Egypt already has a military presence there "at the request of our brothers and sisters" and is "strengthening that." He said they are protecting sensitive sites, including holy sites, but gave no numbers. Unnamed sources told The National the troops number in the hundreds, backed by armor and air-defense batteries including the locally made Amoun system, and that the mission is defensive rather than an offensive against the Houthis. Parliament authorized the deployment in October, after President Abdel Fattah El-Sisi offered military help if asked. Saudi Arabia has faced repeated Houthi missile and drone attacks on airports and oil sites, and Riyadh has not commented publicly on the Egyptian role.

**Record remittances:** Egyptians working abroad sent home $13 billion in July-September, up 20.4% from a year earlier, the central bank said, taking the total for the first nine months of 2026 to a record $38.2 billion, against about $30.2 billion a year earlier. The flow has held up through the war-driven swings in the pound, even though a payments firm had warned in June that Gulf-based workers were running down their savings. Remittances reached $47.3 billion in the 2025/26 fiscal year and are now the economy's biggest single source of foreign currency, covering much of a trade deficit that widened to $64.8 billion. They matter to millions of households that depend on relatives in the Gulf, and to a pound that leans on steady dollar inflows.

**Minister summoned:** Parliament's transport committee called Transport Minister Kamel El-Wazir to an emergency session today to answer for Sunday's collision at Al-Ayyat in Giza, which killed seven people, including the driver of the Fayoum-bound train, and injured 125. Some lawmakers have called for his resignation. Prosecutors have already detained two railway officials after finding that the train's automatic control system had been switched off. The Cairo-High Dam line reopened on Tuesday, restoring the main rail link to Upper Egypt for thousands of daily passengers.

**Brotherhood listing made permanent:** Egypt moved the Muslim Brotherhood from a temporary to a permanent listing as a terrorist entity, in a prosecution decision published in the official gazette on November 8, citing final rulings by the Cairo Criminal Court and the Court of Cassation. The listing bans the group's activities, meetings and fundraising and keeps its assets frozen; three affiliated men were added permanently to the list of individual terrorists, which brings travel bans and asset freezes. The group was first declared a terrorist organization in December 2013.

**Jobs in Germany:** Germany's state of Saxony plans to recruit qualified Egyptians in eight sectors, including nursing and medical care, industry, technology, construction, transport, education, social services and hospitality, following talks with the foreign ministry. Egypt has proposed a steering committee and a task force to run the scheme; numbers, eligibility and application dates have not yet been announced.

**Antiquities pact renewed:** Egypt and the United States renewed for five years an agreement that keeps US import restrictions on Egyptian archaeological objects and backs joint work against trafficking. The deal, first signed in 2016, has helped return more than 5,000 artifacts over the past decade, including a stela from the Temple of Hatshepsut missing since 1930.

## Worth Knowing

**Credit ratings: why a letter grade sets a country's borrowing bill:** A sovereign credit rating is an agency's opinion of how likely a government is to repay its debts in full and on time. S&P, Moody's and Fitch use letter scales, from AAA at the top down through the BBB band, the lowest "investment grade," and into the B and C bands, known as junk. Many pension and insurance funds may only buy investment-grade bonds, so where a country sits on the scale shapes who can lend to it and at what price.

Investors express that risk as a spread: the extra yield a country pays over US Treasuries. Take a government issuing a $1 billion, five-year dollar bond. If a one-notch upgrade narrows its spread by half a percentage point, from 6% to 5.5% over Treasuries, it saves $5 million a year in interest, or $25 million over the bond's life. Across tens of billions of dollars of foreign debt, the gains add up fast.

An outlook (positive, stable or negative) signals the likely direction of the next move, and a "watch" signals a decision within weeks. Ratings follow a few core measures: debt and deficits, foreign-currency reserves, growth, inflation and the stability of institutions.

African governments have long argued that the big three penalize them for thin data, which is why a new African Credit Rating Agency, backed by Egypt's central bank, aims to issue its first sovereign ratings by the end of March 2027. Its views will count only once investors trust them.
