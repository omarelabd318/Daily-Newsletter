## Egypt News

**Land for debt:** A presidential decree published in the Official Gazette lets the Finance Ministry use about 26,690 feddans of state land in the Red Sea governorate, some 112 square kilometers, to reduce public debt as well as to back sovereign sukuk. A June allocation had reserved the plot, near Zafarana, for sukuk only. On the same day, Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly reviewed with Finance Minister Ahmed Kouchouk a plan to cut public debt to about 69.5% of GDP by 2030, with fixed ceilings on debt and state guarantees. Budget-sector debt stood at EGP 17.85 trillion, or 83.9% of GDP, at the end of March. Less debt means less of the budget goes to interest, leaving more for services.

> **Explained: sovereign sukuk.** Islamic bonds issued by a government that pay investors from the income or use of a specific asset, such as land, rather than fixed interest.

**Faculty pay rise:** All faculty members at Egypt's public universities will get a salary increase, the cabinet said after Madbouly met Kouchouk and Higher Education Minister Abdelaziz Konsowa, with the size and cost to be announced at a press conference on Monday, November 16. It comes on top of a EGP 1,000 monthly teaching allowance approved last week, the first step of a three-year plan to lift academic pay.

**Minimum pension rises in January:** The minimum pension is set to rise to EGP 2,080 a month in January 2027, from EGP 1,755 this year, an increase of about 18.5%, under plans set out by the head of the National Organization for Social Insurance at the Egypt Forum 2026. About 380,000 of Egypt's roughly 11.5 million pensioners and beneficiaries receive the minimum.

**Terror lists widened:** A Cairo criminal court placed 35 organizations and 36 individuals, most of them based in Europe, on Egypt's terrorist lists for five years. They include the Council of European Muslims, the Muslim Association of Britain and the European Council for Fatwa and Research. The ruling follows the Muslim Brotherhood's permanent listing on November 8; listings bring asset freezes and travel bans.

**Mada Masr editor's retrial:** A New Cairo court postponed to November 18 the retrial of Lina Attalah, editor of the independent news site Mada Masr, on charges of publishing false news and running an unlicensed website. The UN human rights office said the case "raises fresh concerns" about due process. An appeal against a separate October 27 ruling that fined Attalah EGP 3 million and ordered the site closed is set for November 25.

**Locally assembled electric car:** The cabinet reviewed plans for the first fully electric car assembled in Egypt, the Dongfeng-brand Mage EV, which SN Automotive will build at Al Nasr Automotive's plant, Youm7 reported. ## Worth Knowing

**Debt-to-GDP: why the ratio matters more than the total:** A government's debt is usually judged against the size of its economy, the debt-to-GDP ratio, because a bigger economy produces more income and taxes to service it. Egypt's target of 69.5% by 2030 means debt worth a little over two-thirds of a year's national output.

The ratio can fall in three ways. The first is a primary surplus: collecting more in revenue than the state spends before interest, so less new borrowing is needed. The second is growth: if nominal GDP, which includes inflation, rises faster than the interest rate on the debt, the ratio shrinks even without repayment. The third is selling or using assets, such as state land or company stakes, to retire debt directly.

Take a simple example. A country owes 85 on an economy of 100, a ratio of 85%. If nominal GDP grows 15% to 115 while debt rises 8%, to about 92, the ratio drops to 80% in a single year, without any cut in the debt itself.

The catch is the interest bill. When rates are high, as they are in Egypt after the October hike, interest can absorb a large share of revenue, which is why each point off the ratio, and each cut in rates, frees money for schools, hospitals and subsidies.
