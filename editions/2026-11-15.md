## Egypt News

- **IMF review goes virtual:** Egypt's final IMF review has moved to online talks after a Cairo mission ended without a staff-level agreement. The mission, from October 25 to November 5, covered the eighth and last review of the $8 billion program. About $2.3 billion from the loan and the fund's climate facility is at stake, and the program expires on December 15, EnterpriseAM reported. IMF spokesperson Julie Kozack said "important progress was made," but gave no timeline and said any follow-up arrangement is "very much for the Egyptian authorities." Some local reports put the loan tranche alone at about $1.5 billion. The money is the last external financing the program offers, and a deal before the deadline would steady the pound and Egypt's borrowing costs.

> **Explained: staff-level agreement.** The deal IMF staff and a government reach on a review's terms; it still needs approval by the fund's executive board before any money is released.

- **Takaful and Karama payouts:** The Social Solidarity Ministry begins paying November's Takaful and Karama cash support today at bank ATMs nationwide, with payments running to the end of the month. The transfers reach about 4.7 million families and cost more than EGP 4 billion a month, Minister Maya Morsi said. The program is the state's main cash safety net for poor households, elderly people and people with disabilities.

- **Industry export targets:** Industry Minister Khaled Hashem said Egypt aims to lift food-industry exports from $6.8 billion to $18-20 billion within four years, and to raise manufacturing's share of the economy from 12.8% to 20% over the same period, calling the goal "a realistic ambition, not a dream." Speaking on MBC Masr, he also said Egypt supplied more than 60% of Europe's fertilizer imports during the Iran war, a claim no other outlet carried. The targets frame the government's push for factory-led growth, which would bring in dollars and jobs.

- **US praises Brotherhood ban:** Massad Boulos, President Trump's senior adviser on Arab and African affairs, said the United States "commends Egypt" for permanently designating the Muslim Brotherhood a terrorist organization, Egypt Today reported. The prosecution's decision to make the listing permanent was published in the Official Gazette on November 8, and Washington's public backing adds weight to Cairo's long campaign against the group.

- **Joint condemnation of hospital strike:** Egypt joined 17 Arab and Muslim-majority countries in condemning "in the strongest terms" an Israeli strike on the Nasser Medical Complex in Khan Younis. Their foreign ministers said the attack broke international humanitarian law and urged Israel to meet its obligations under the Sharm El-Sheikh Agreement, the US peace plan and UN Security Council Resolution 2803. Egypt is a guarantor of the Gaza ceasefire.

- **E-signatures recognized by the EU:** Egypt has become the first country outside Europe to align its electronic-signature rules with the European Union, under an arrangement signed on November 13 between the IT Industry Development Agency (ITIDA) and the European Commission. EU states will be able to validate Egyptian digital signatures and seals, easing paperwork for trade with Egypt's largest trading partner, which accounts for about a quarter of its foreign trade.

- **Ambassador nominee on the Nile:** Nick Oberheiden, nominated as US ambassador to Egypt, told a Senate committee that Egypt is the United States' "largest export market in Africa," with more than 2,100 US companies operating there, and that President Trump "understands the deep significance of the Nile River for Egypt," EnterpriseAM reported. The committee has not voted, and the nomination, pending since June, may stall when Democrats take control of the committee in January.

## Worth Knowing

**Why bond prices fall when yields rise.** A bond pays a fixed coupon, so when new bonds start paying more, older ones become less attractive and their price has to drop until their return matches the market's. The jump in the US 10-year yield to 5.15% this week is that process at work: holders of existing Treasuries lost money even though the government will pay them in full.

**Duration measures the sensitivity.** A bond's duration, in years, is roughly the percentage its price changes for each one-point move in yields. Take a 10-year Treasury with a duration of about 8: a rise in its yield from 4.86% to 5.15%, or 0.29 points, cuts its price by about 8 × 0.29 = 2.3%. A one-year Egyptian treasury bill, with a duration under one, would lose only a fraction of that for the same move, which is one reason short-term bills attract investors when rates are volatile.

**What it means for savers.** Rising yields hurt existing bondholders but reward new buyers, who lock in higher income. For anyone choosing between a long, fixed-rate investment and a short one, the trade-off is the same: longer terms pay more when rates stay put, but lose more when rates rise.
