## Egypt News

**Egypt and Libya sign deals:** Egypt and Libya signed agreements on gas infrastructure, fisheries and labor on Thursday as President Abdel Fattah El-Sisi received Abdul Hamid Dbeibah, prime minister of Libya's Tripoli-based Government of National Unity. The visit came a day after Cairo rejected the 2019 maritime memorandum between Turkey and Libya: Egypt's UN mission sent Secretary-General António Guterres a note calling it null, and spokesman Nader Zaki said it "does not bind Egypt in any way." Why it matters: Libya is a major employer of Egyptian workers and a growing energy partner, while the maritime dispute touches Egypt's own gas claims in the eastern Mediterranean. The values of the new deals were not disclosed.

**Trade deficit widens:** Egypt's trade deficit widened 60.6% year on year in September to about $5.3 billion, the statistics agency CAPMAS said, as imports rose 29.7% to $10.6 billion while exports grew only 7.4% to $5.3 billion, Arab Finance reported. The gap was still narrower than August's $6.4 billion. Why it matters: a wider deficit means more demand for dollars, a pressure on the pound that remittances, Suez Canal fees and tourism must offset.

**$38.2 billion in debt service:** Egypt paid $38.2 billion on its external debt in fiscal 2025/26, the year to June, according to the central bank's monthly bulletin released on Thursday: $30.4 billion in principal and $7.8 billion in interest, Masrawy reported. Why it matters: repayments on that scale absorb a large share of the country's foreign currency, which is why the government is working to stretch out maturities and cut short-term borrowing.

**$3 billion in new issues:** Finance Minister Ahmed Kouchouk said in Athens on Thursday that the government plans $3 billion of international issuances to diversify its funding sources. He said the primary surplus reached 4.9% of GDP last year, with 5% targeted this year, and that inflation has fallen from 35% to about 13% in two years. He gave no instrument or timing.

> **Explained: primary surplus.** What a government has left after paying for everything except interest on its debt. A surplus shows the budget would balance before borrowing costs, which reassures lenders.

**Mina West starts early:** Production began at the Mina West gas field in the Mediterranean two months ahead of schedule, Petroleum Minister Karim Badawi said on Thursday. Its two wells add about 160 million cubic feet of gas a day and about 3,000 barrels of condensate. Why it matters: each new field trims the liquefied gas Egypt must import in dollars to keep power stations and factories running.

> **Explained: condensate.** A light liquid oil that comes up with natural gas from some fields. It is sold like crude, so it adds export or refining value on top of the gas itself.

## Top World Headlines

**Shenzhen declaration:** Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin ended the first three-way summit of their countries' leaders in Shenzhen on Thursday with a four-point "peace declaration" covering UN reform, oil supply, dialogue on nuclear risk and what it called the International Criminal Court's "jurisdiction abuse." It left out Ukraine and Iran and set no arms cuts. Trump said the three were "uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth." Why it matters: the summit produced warm words but no deal on the two wars that drive oil prices.

**Russia strikes Kyiv:** Russian missiles and drones hit Kyiv overnight into Friday, killing at least three people and wounding 15, the mayor said, and damaged three bridges over the Dnipro; two more people were killed in the Lviv region, AFP reported. Putin had promised a "much more painful" response after Ukraine's strikes on the Crimean bridge this week.

**NATO for Donbas:** US envoys floated NATO membership for Ukraine in exchange for Kyiv giving up the Donbas region, the Wall Street Journal reported, and President Volodymyr Zelenskiy confirmed the idea had been raised. Russia's Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov pushed back on Friday against any NATO-style guarantees for Ukraine, according to Reuters.

**Rohingya genocide ruling:** The International Court of Justice found on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya Muslim minority, the first time the court has held a state responsible for genocide, in a case Gambia brought in 2019. More than 700,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh in 2017. The EU said "compliance with ICJ's decisions is not optional."

**UK-EU summit:** Prime Minister Andy Burnham meets the EU's top officials in Brussels on Friday for a summit he has called a step toward undoing the damage of Brexit, saying "the Brexit we were given has not worked." He has said the new deals could add about 0.4% to Britain's economy.

**COP31 draft weakens:** A revised draft at the COP31 climate talks in Antalya, cut to nine pages from 18, dropped language on moving away from fossil fuels and a target of $120 billion a year in adaptation finance by 2035, while keeping goals to triple renewables and reach a 35% electricity share of energy by 2035. Talks continue.

**Iran war vote fails:** The US Senate voted 51-48 on Thursday to reject a House-passed resolution that would have ended US involvement in the war with Iran, in the first vote on it since the November 3 midterms. Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Rand Paul voted for it.

## Worth Knowing

**Why rate fears hit growth stocks hardest:** A share is worth the cash a company is expected to earn in future, brought back to today's money with a discount rate that rises with interest rates. The further away the cash, the more a higher rate shrinks its present value.

**An example:** Take two companies each expected to pay EGP 100. A mature firm pays it next year; a fast-growing tech firm pays it in 10 years. At a 5% discount rate, the first is worth about EGP 95 today and the second about EGP 61. Raise the rate to 6% and the first slips to about EGP 94, barely 1% less, while the second drops to about EGP 56, a fall of about 9%.

**Why it matters now:** That is why talk of a December Fed hike, with 10-year Treasury yields near 5%, hit chip and AI stocks such as Nvidia and AMD far harder than Walmart or Berkshire Hathaway on Thursday. Much of their value rests on profits expected years from now.

**The takeaway:** Long-dated assets, whether growth stocks or long-term bonds, are the most sensitive to rate changes. Investors who hold them should expect bigger swings whenever the outlook for rates shifts, in either direction.
