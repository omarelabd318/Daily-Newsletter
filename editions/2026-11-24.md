## Egypt News

**Egypt and Iraq:** Egypt and Iraq agreed on Monday to lift entry visas between the two countries, Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi announced after talks with President Abdel Fattah El-Sisi on his first official visit to Cairo. Iraq is also weighing supplying Egypt with about 10 million barrels of crude, including by routes that avoid the Strait of Hormuz, Iraqi trade official Sattar Al Jabri told the Iraqi News Agency, as reported by EnterpriseAM; no volume, price or signed contract has been announced. The two prime ministers chaired the fourth meeting of their joint higher committee and met Egyptian business leaders. No start date for visa-free travel was given. The deal matters for a country that has turned away LNG cargoes this month as European buyers outbid it: a second crude supplier outside the Gulf chokepoint would ease pressure on fuel imports.

**Private sector projects:** A decision by Prime Minister Moustafa Madbouly, in force since Monday, requires every state body to report the projects it wants to open to private investors, with their cost, location and tender dates, to the statistics agency CAPMAS and the Finance Ministry's public-private partnership unit by January 1, and then every quarter. The unit will publish the pipeline. The aim is a single, public list of opportunities for investors, a long-standing request from business groups as the state pledges to step back from parts of the economy.

**Tap-to-pay on Android:** The Central Bank of Egypt extended card tokenization to Android phones on Monday, letting holders of Egyptian bank cards pay contactless with their phones. Since the service launched on iPhones in December 2024, it has handled about 292 million transactions worth EGP 228 billion to the end of September. Android runs on most phones in Egypt, so the move widens mobile payments to most smartphone users and supports the push to cut cash use.

> **Explained: card tokenization.** Replacing a card's real number with a stand-in code stored on the phone, so a payment never exposes the card details themselves.

**Free zones in Upper Egypt:** Investment Minister Mohamed Farid outlined plans for three new public free zones in Beni Suef, Sohag and Aswan, covering 432 feddans, with about $1.9 billion of investment, 272 projects and roughly 42,000 jobs expected. No timetable was given. Free zones let exporters import inputs duty-free, and the plan is part of a drive to bring industry and jobs to the south, where poverty rates are highest.

**Fiscal space target:** Finance Minister Ahmed Kouchouk said the government aims to "gradually double" its fiscal space, the money left for spending after fixed obligations, to EGP 4.8 trillion by fiscal year 2029/30 without new burdens on citizens, speaking at the Global Forum on Social Protection on Monday. The pledge is a signal for social spending, but the ministry did not give the base figure it is doubling from.

## Worth Knowing

**How an IPO price range works.** When a company sells shares to the public for the first time, its advisers rarely announce a single price up front. Instead they set a range and run a "book-building": big investors say how many shares they want and at what price inside the range, and the final price is set once the book closes, usually where demand is strong enough to sell every share.

Misr Life Insurance shows the arithmetic. It is selling 750 million shares at EGP 12.00 to 13.60 each. At the bottom, the sale raises 750 million × 12.00 = EGP 9.0 billion; at the top, 750 million × 13.60 = EGP 10.2 billion. Because those shares are 15% of the company, the range values the whole insurer at EGP 60 to 68 billion.

An independent adviser put fair value at EGP 13.82 a share. Against that, the top of the range is a discount of about 1.6% and the bottom about 13.2%. Sellers offer such a discount on purpose: it leaves room for the price to rise after listing, which rewards early buyers and helps future sales.

The catch is that fair value is an estimate, not a promise. If markets fall or demand is weak, a listed share can trade below both its fair value and its IPO price. The discount gives buyers a margin of safety, but only as wide as the estimate is right.
